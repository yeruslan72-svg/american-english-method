Nelle Harper Lee (1926–2016) was an American novelist from Monroeville, Alabama. She became internationally known for *To Kill a Mockingbird*, published in 1960. The novel won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction in 1961 and became one of the most widely read works of modern American literature.

Lee was a private person who rarely gave interviews. She published essays and other short pieces, but her two novels remain the centre of her literary reputation: *To Kill a Mockingbird* (1960) and *Go Set a Watchman* (2015).

Her writing explores childhood, moral responsibility, prejudice, justice, memory, and the complicated relationship between what people believe and how they behave.

## 2. From Monroeville to New York

Harper Lee was born on April 28, 1926, in Monroeville, a small town in southwest Alabama. Her father, Amasa Coleman Lee, was a lawyer and newspaper editor who also served in the Alabama legislature. Her childhood friend and neighbour was Truman Capote, who later became a well-known writer.

Lee studied at Huntingdon College and the University of Alabama. She wrote for student publications and studied law for a time, but she did not complete a law degree. In 1949 she moved to New York City, where she worked as an airline reservations clerk while writing in her spare time.

Friends gave her financial support that allowed her to leave her job and concentrate on a manuscript. With the guidance of editor Tay Hohoff, she reshaped a series of connected stories into the novel that became *To Kill a Mockingbird*.

In 1959, Lee travelled to Kansas with Truman Capote and assisted him with research for *In Cold Blood*. Capote acknowledged her contribution in the book's dedication.

## 3. The World Behind Maycomb

*To Kill a Mockingbird* is set in the fictional town of Maycomb, Alabama, during the Great Depression of the 1930s. The town reflects aspects of the small Southern community where Lee grew up, but Maycomb is a fictional place.

The story takes place under Jim Crow, a system of laws and customs that enforced racial segregation and discrimination across much of the American South. Black Americans faced unequal treatment in education, public life, employment, housing, and the justice system. Racial violence and intimidation were part of this history.

The novel examines this world through the experiences of children who gradually discover that the adults around them do not always act fairly. It is important to understand the historical setting rather than treat the story as a timeless or complete account of race relations in America.

The old courthouse in Monroeville is one of the real places associated with the novel. Harper Lee's father practised law there, and the courtroom helped inspire the fictional courtroom in the book. The Hollywood film recreated the courtroom on a studio set; it was not filmed inside the Monroeville courthouse.

## 4. To Kill a Mockingbird (1960)

The novel is narrated by Jean Louise “Scout” Finch, looking back on her childhood. She lives with her older brother Jem and their father, Atticus Finch. Their neighbour Dill joins them during the summer.

The children become fascinated by the mysterious Arthur “Boo” Radley, who rarely leaves his house. At the same time, their father takes on the defence of Tom Robinson, a Black man accused of assaulting a white woman.

The story brings together childhood curiosity, family life, local gossip, fear, courage, and a deeply unequal legal system. The two strands gradually reveal how people judge others without understanding them.

### Meet the Main Characters

**Scout Finch**

Jean Louise “Scout” is the narrator. She is curious, outspoken, energetic, and often impatient with rules she does not understand. Through her eyes, readers encounter the town's contradictions and watch a child develop a more complex understanding of people.

**Jem Finch**

Jem is Scout's older brother. As he grows older, he begins to understand that the world is more complicated and less fair than he once believed. His reactions to the trial and its aftermath are an important part of the novel.

**Atticus Finch**

Atticus is a lawyer and the father of Scout and Jem. He tries to teach his children to think carefully, listen to others, and act according to conscience. His decision to defend Tom Robinson places him in conflict with many people in the town.

Atticus is an influential literary character, but readers can also discuss the limits of the novel's perspective and the way Black characters are represented.

**Tom Robinson**

Tom Robinson is a Black man accused of a crime he did not commit. His trial exposes the racial prejudice built into the town's social and legal structures. His story is central to the novel's examination of justice and inequality.

**Calpurnia**

Calpurnia is the Finch family's housekeeper, who helps care for Scout and Jem. She has her own family and community life, and she gives the children an important view of the world beyond their immediate household.

**Arthur “Boo” Radley**

Boo is a reclusive neighbour whom the children turn into a figure of mystery. As the story develops, they learn that rumours and fear can hide a person's real character.

**Dill Harris**

Dill is a friend who spends summers with Scout and Jem. His imagination and questions bring energy to the children's adventures.

### What Does the Mockingbird Symbolise?

The mockingbird becomes a symbol of innocence and harmlessness. The novel asks readers to consider the damage people can do when they harm those who have done them no harm.

The symbol connects different parts of the story, but it does not replace the historical and social realities the novel explores.

## 5. Go Set a Watchman (2015)

*Go Set a Watchman* was published in 2015. It follows an adult Jean Louise Finch as she returns to Maycomb and confronts changes in her family and hometown.

The manuscript was written before *To Kill a Mockingbird*, although it was published much later. It is therefore not simply a conventional sequel written after the first novel. Its publication prompted debate about its relationship to the earlier book, its portrayal of familiar characters, and the circumstances surrounding the manuscript's release.

For readers, the two books raise different questions. *To Kill a Mockingbird* presents childhood memory and moral awakening; *Go Set a Watchman* explores the difficult process of reassessing people and beliefs as an adult.

A useful reading approach is to read *To Kill a Mockingbird* first, then approach *Go Set a Watchman* as a later-published, earlier-written manuscript with a different perspective.

## 6. Themes Worth Exploring

- **Conscience and responsibility:** What should a person do when doing the right thing is unpopular?
- **Prejudice and injustice:** How do stereotypes and unequal institutions affect people's lives?
- **Childhood and growing up:** How do Scout and Jem change as they learn more about their community?
- **Empathy and perspective:** What does it mean to try to understand another person's experience?
- **Rumour and fear:** How can a community create a false image of someone it does not know?
- **Family and community:** How do family values interact with the expectations of a town?
- **Memory and change:** How does an adult narrator understand events from childhood?

The novel is often praised for encouraging empathy, but it is also discussed critically. Readers can examine whose experiences are central, whose voices are less developed, and how the story frames race and justice. A thoughtful reading makes room for both the novel's influence and serious questions about its perspective.

## 7. Language in Context

These are natural contemporary American English examples for learners. They are not quotations from Harper Lee's novels.

### Talking About Fairness and Responsibility

- **That's not fair.** — That's not fair, and everyone knows it.
- **to stand up for someone** — She stood up for her friend.
- **to do the right thing** — He tried to do the right thing, even when it was difficult.
- **to treat someone fairly** — Everyone deserves to be treated fairly.
- **to judge someone** — Don't judge someone before you know the facts.
- **to speak up** — Someone needs to speak up when something is wrong.
- **to take responsibility** — We all have to take responsibility for our actions.
- **to look at it from someone else's point of view** — Try to look at it from her point of view.

### Describing People and First Impressions

- **There's more to someone than meets the eye.**
- **You can't judge a book by its cover.**
- **I got the wrong impression.**
- **I didn't know the whole story.**
- **It made me see things differently.**
- **He turned out to be different from what I expected.**

### Useful Vocabulary

| Word or phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| conscience | the sense of right and wrong that guides a person |
| fairness | treating people in an equal and reasonable way |
| prejudice | an unfair opinion formed without enough knowledge |
| discrimination | unfair treatment based on identity or background |
| justice | fair treatment and the proper application of the law |
| courage | the ability to face fear or difficulty |
| empathy | the ability to understand another person's feelings or experience |
| to defend | to speak or act in support of someone; in law, to represent an accused person |
| to accuse | to say that someone has done something wrong |
| to testify | to give evidence in court |
| to belong | to feel accepted as part of a group or place |
| to stand up for | to support or defend a person, idea, or principle |

## 10. Put It Into Practice

### Speaking: Introduce the Novel

Give a one-minute introduction to *To Kill a Mockingbird*. Include the setting, the narrator, the central conflict, and one theme that interests you.

### Speaking: Describe a Character

Choose Scout, Jem, Atticus, Calpurnia, or Boo Radley. Describe the character's personality, how they change or affect the story, and what you find interesting about them.

### Writing: A Short Response

Write 120–150 words on this question:

**What does it mean to stand up for what you believe is right?**

Use at least three expressions from Section 7.

### Everyday Transfer

Think of a situation in which someone was judged unfairly or misunderstood. Explain what happened, how the people involved felt, and what could have been done differently. Do not include private details about real people.

## 13. Your Takeaway

Harper Lee's writing invites readers to look beyond first impressions and think about conscience, justice, and the responsibility people have toward one another. *To Kill a Mockingbird* is rooted in a particular time and place, but its questions continue to prompt discussion.

Read with curiosity. Pay attention to the historical context. And when you express an opinion, support it with details from the story.
