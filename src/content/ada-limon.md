# Ada Limón (1976– )

## 1. Who Is Ada Limón?

Ada Limón is a contemporary American poet whose work brings together the natural world, family history, memory, love, vulnerability, and the experience of being alive. She was born on March 28, 1976, in Sonoma, California, and is of Mexican ancestry. Her mother, Stacia Brady, is an artist; Limón has spoken about the influence of visual art on the way she observes and describes the world.

Limón earned a Master of Fine Arts degree from New York University's Creative Writing Program in 2001. Her early books include *This Big Fake World* and *Lucky Wreck*. Later collections brought wider recognition: *Bright Dead Things* (2015) was a finalist for the National Book Award, while *The Carrying* (2018) won the National Book Critics Circle Award for Poetry. *The Hurting Kind* (2022) was shortlisted for the Griffin Poetry Prize.

In July 2022, Librarian of Congress Carla Hayden appointed Limón the twenty-fourth Poet Laureate of the United States. She served two terms, from 2022 until April 2025. Her signature project, **You Are Here**, invited people to reconnect with the natural world through poetry. It included a 2024 anthology of new nature poems and poetry installations in seven U.S. national parks.

Limón also wrote *In Praise of Mystery: A Poem for Europa*, a poem created in collaboration with NASA and engraved on the Europa Clipper spacecraft, launched in October 2024. Her more recent collection is *Startlement: New & Selected Poems* (2025).

## 2. A Poet Who Notices the Living World

Nature in Limón's poetry is not simply beautiful scenery. Trees, birds, animals, gardens, weather, and changing seasons can become ways to think about grief, recovery, family, time, and belonging.

Her poems often begin with an ordinary observation: a tree changing colour, an animal moving, a familiar landscape, or a moment between family members. The observation gradually opens into a larger emotional or philosophical question.

Some recurring concerns in her work include:

- **Attention:** learning to look closely at ordinary things.
- **The natural world:** plants, animals, seasons, landscapes, and the relationship between people and nature.
- **Family and memory:** how childhood and family relationships shape adult life.
- **The body:** vulnerability, illness, ageing, strength, and the experience of being human.
- **Resilience:** continuing to live and care despite pain, uncertainty, or loss.
- **Connection:** the ways people, animals, and places are linked.

## 3. Style and Language

Limón's poems are accessible in the sense that they often begin with recognisable experiences and clear images. However, their meanings can be layered. A natural image may carry emotional, social, or philosophical significance.

Notice these features:

- **Concrete imagery:** specific colours, plants, animals, sounds, and physical details help readers picture a scene.
- **Metaphor:** an image from nature can suggest an emotional experience or a human condition.
- **Enjambment:** a sentence continues across a line break, creating movement or a moment of surprise.
- **Free verse:** many poems do not follow a fixed rhyme scheme or regular metre.
- **Conversational voice:** the speaker may sound intimate, reflective, direct, or quietly humorous.
- **Emotional movement:** a poem can move from a small observation toward a wider realisation without explaining everything.

When reading Limón, do not assume that a tree, bird, or animal has only one symbolic meaning. First notice what is literally happening. Then ask what the image makes the speaker feel or understand.

## 4. Important Collections and Poems

### *Bright Dead Things* (2015)

This collection was a finalist for the National Book Award. It explores identity, memory, family, desire, and the complicated process of becoming an adult. One of its best-known poems is “How to Triumph Like a Girl,” which uses the image of female racehorses to explore strength and confidence.

### *The Carrying* (2018)

Winner of the National Book Critics Circle Award for Poetry. The collection considers family, love, the body, grief, and the natural world. “Instructions on Not Giving Up” is a particularly useful poem for studying how a close observation of spring growth can develop into a reflection on resilience.

### *The Hurting Kind* (2022)

This collection continues Limón's attention to animals, plants, family, memory, and the fragility of life. It was shortlisted for the Griffin Poetry Prize.

### *You Are Here: Poetry in the Natural World* (2024)

An anthology edited by Limón during her laureateship. It brings together new poems by living American poets and reflects her interest in how poetry can help people pay attention to the places where they live.

### *Startlement: New & Selected Poems* (2025)

A substantial selection of earlier work together with new poems. It offers readers a way to trace the development of Limón's poetic voice across several collections.

## 5. Guided Reading: “Instructions on Not Giving Up”

**Poem:** “Instructions on Not Giving Up”  
**Collection:** *The Carrying* (2018)  
**Original publication:** Academy of American Poets, Poem-a-Day, May 15, 2017.

[Read the complete poem at the Academy of American Poets](https://poets.org/anthology/resilience-1/embed). The page includes the poem and an audio player. The text remains copyrighted by Ada Limón; it is linked here for lawful reading and listening, not reproduced on this educational site.

### What happens in the poem?

The speaker begins by noticing the flowering trees of spring. The bright blossoms are striking, but the deeper change is the gradual return of green leaves. The poem then connects this patient growth with the possibility of continuing after difficult experiences.

The poem does not describe recovery as instant or effortless. Its image of new leaves suggests persistence: life continues, even after a hard season. The natural world becomes a source of reflection, not a simple promise that everything will be easy.

### Close-reading guide

1. **Opening observation:** Identify the details that make the spring scene vivid. Which colours and shapes can you imagine?
2. **Change of focus:** Notice how the poem moves from blossoms to leaves. Why might the slower, greener change matter more to the speaker?
3. **Seasonal metaphor:** Winter can suggest a difficult period, while new growth can suggest renewal. What makes this comparison effective?
4. **Tone:** The speaker's voice is observant and conversational, but the poem gradually becomes more reflective.
5. **Ending:** Consider the final image of a leaf opening. Does it suggest strength, acceptance, or both? Explain your answer using the poem.

### Literary devices to notice

- **Visual imagery:** colours and plant forms make the scene concrete.
- **Metaphor:** seasonal growth becomes a way to think about emotional survival.
- **Personification:** the natural world is presented as if it can respond or speak.
- **Contrast:** spectacular blossoms are set against the quieter, more persistent greening of leaves.
- **Enjambment:** line breaks carry the reader forward and shape the pace of thought.

## 6. Another Poem: “How to Triumph Like a Girl”

This poem appears in *Bright Dead Things* (2015). It begins with the speaker's admiration for female racehorses and develops into a bold celebration of power, confidence, and shared identity.

[Read “How to Triumph Like a Girl” at the Academy of American Poets](https://poets.org/anthology/poems-i-118). The anthology page includes the poem and an audio player. The poem is copyrighted and is provided here through a link to its authorised publication.

### Reading questions

1. Why does the speaker focus on female horses?
2. How does the poem challenge expectations about delicacy and strength?
3. What effect does the energetic, conversational voice create?
4. How does the central image connect the animal world with human identity?
5. Which words or images make the poem feel confident and celebratory?

### Compare the two poems

“Instructions on Not Giving Up” uses the gradual growth of leaves to consider resilience. “How to Triumph Like a Girl” uses the power and movement of racehorses to celebrate strength. Both poems draw meaning from close attention to the natural world, but their energy and tone are different.

## 8. “You Are Here”: Poetry in the Natural World

As U.S. Poet Laureate, Limón developed **You Are Here**, a project about the relationship between poetry and the natural world. One part was an anthology of new nature poetry. Another part, **Poetry in Parks**, brought poetry installations to seven national parks.

The project encouraged visitors to slow down and notice their surroundings. It also invited people who do not usually read poetry to write in response to the landscape around them.

Limón's collaboration with NASA extended this idea beyond Earth. Her poem *In Praise of Mystery: A Poem for Europa* was engraved on the Europa Clipper spacecraft. The mission launched in October 2024 to study Jupiter's moon Europa.

### A short creative activity

Look out of a window or remember a familiar outdoor place. Write five lines answering these prompts:

- What is the first thing you notice?
- What is moving?
- What sound can you hear?
- What has changed since another season?
- What does this place make you feel or remember?

Do not try to write a perfect poem. Begin with accurate observation.
