# Meet Charles Yu: Who Gets to Be the Main Character?

**21st-Century American Writers · Charles Yu · born 1976**

Charles Yu is an American novelist, short-story writer, and television screenwriter. His work often combines science fiction, humour, and sharp social observation. His novel *Interior Chinatown* (2020), written in the form of a screenplay, won the National Book Award for Fiction. It asks who gets to tell a story, who is allowed to become its hero, and how stereotypes shape the roles people are expected to play.

---

## 1. Who Is Charles Yu?

Charles Yu was born in Los Angeles in 1976 and grew up in a Taiwanese American family. He studied molecular and cellular biology at the University of California, Berkeley, while also pursuing creative writing. Later, he earned a law degree from Columbia Law School.

Before becoming a full-time writer, Yu worked as a lawyer and held business and legal roles in the technology and entertainment industries. His professional path was unusual: science, law, business, fiction, and television all became part of his experience as a writer.

Yu has written novels and short stories as well as scripts for television. He worked on the HBO series *Westworld* and developed projects for several television and film companies. His novel *Interior Chinatown* was adapted into a television series released in 2024; Yu served as its creator and showrunner.

His career is a reminder that a writer's voice can grow from many kinds of experience. A background in science or law does not prevent someone from becoming an imaginative storyteller.

## 2. Interior Chinatown: A Life Written Like a Screenplay

Published in 2020, *Interior Chinatown* follows Willis Wu, a Taiwanese American man who works as a background actor in a fictional police television show called *Black and White*. Willis is repeatedly assigned minor, stereotyped parts. He dreams of moving beyond the background and becoming a central character in his own life.

The novel is not written like a conventional novel. It uses screenplay conventions: scene headings, character names, stage directions, and dialogue. This form is important to the meaning of the story. Willis's world seems to operate like a television production in which people are assigned roles before they have a chance to define themselves.

The book moves between the fictional show, Willis's family life, and his growing awareness of the limits placed on him. Its humour can be sharp, but the story also explores the emotional cost of being treated as a stereotype rather than as a complete person.

### What Is a Screenplay?

A screenplay is a script written for a film or television production. It usually identifies scenes, describes actions, and presents dialogue under character names. Yu uses this familiar format as a literary device: the structure helps readers see how a person's identity can be shaped by the roles society offers.

### Why the Form Matters

The screenplay format is not just decoration. It makes the reader notice the machinery of storytelling: who gets dialogue, who stays in the background, who is given a name, and who is reduced to a type. The form becomes part of the argument.

## 3. Stereotypes, Roles, and Representation

A **stereotype** is an oversimplified idea about a group of people. In entertainment, stereotypes can limit characters to a narrow set of expected roles. *Interior Chinatown* examines how Asian American characters have often been represented in film and television, especially when they are treated as background figures rather than complex individuals.

The novel does not ask readers to see Willis only as a victim. It gives him ambition, humour, family relationships, contradictions, and the desire to make choices. The central question is larger than getting a better acting part: can a person claim authorship of their own story?

Useful questions while reading:

- Who assigns a role to Willis?
- How does the language of television shape the way he sees himself?
- When does humour expose an unfair assumption?
- What changes when a background character becomes the focus?

## 4. Other Works: Science Fiction and Short Stories

Yu's first novel, *How to Live Safely in a Science Fictional Universe* (2010), is a playful and thoughtful work of speculative fiction. It follows a time-machine repair technician who becomes involved in a journey through time, memory, and family history. The novel uses science-fiction ideas to explore regret, identity, and the wish to change the past.

His short-story collections include *Third Class Superhero* (2006) and *Sorry Please Thank You* (2012). These stories show his interest in unusual premises, unexpected shifts, and the emotional lives hidden inside ordinary situations.

Together, these works reveal a writer who uses imaginative forms to ask familiar human questions: How do we understand our past? What makes a life meaningful? Can we escape the stories other people tell about us?

## 5. From Page to Screen: The 2024 Television Adaptation

Yu created and served as showrunner of the television adaptation of *Interior Chinatown*. The ten-episode series premiered in 2024. It stars Jimmy O. Yang as Willis Wu, with Ronny Chieng, Chloe Bennet, and other performers.

A screen adaptation is not identical to a novel. A book can use typography, narration, and the reader's imagination; television uses actors, images, editing, sound, and performance. Comparing the two versions offers a useful way to think about how stories change when they move between media.

## 6. Language in Context

| Word or expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| background actor | a performer who appears in a scene without being the main focus |
| stereotype | an oversimplified and often unfair idea about a group |
| screenplay | a script written for a film or television programme |
| role | a character or part played by an actor; also a person's function |
| representation | the way a person or group is shown in art, media, or public life |
| to be typecast | to be repeatedly given the same kind of role |
| showrunner | the person who leads the creative and practical work of a television series |
| point of view | the position or perspective from which a story is told |
| to take centre stage | to become the main focus of attention |
| to define oneself | to decide and express who you are, rather than accept others' labels |

### Useful Expressions

- **in the background** — not receiving attention or playing a central role
- **to get a speaking part** — to receive a role with dialogue
- **to break the mould** — to do something in a new way instead of following expectations
- **to tell your own story** — to express your experience in your own words
- **to be more than a label** — to be more complex than a category or stereotype

## 10. Compare the Writers

Charles Yu's work can be read alongside other contemporary authors on this site:

- **Percival Everett** also uses satire and inventive storytelling to question how American culture creates and repeats narratives.
- **Tess Gunty** builds a many-voiced portrait of a community, asking readers to look beyond first impressions.
- **Tommy Orange** explores identity, representation, and contemporary Native American life through intersecting voices.
- **Charles Yu** focuses on roles, stereotypes, media, and the possibility of taking control of one's own story.

These writers have different backgrounds and styles, but all invite readers to question whose experiences are visible and whose voices are heard.

---

## A Final Thought

A person is never just a background figure, a label, or a role assigned by someone else. Charles Yu uses the language of television and fiction to remind readers that every life contains a story more complicated than the part others expect us to play.
