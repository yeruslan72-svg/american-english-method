# Meet John Steinbeck: Stories of Ordinary People

**American Writers · John Steinbeck · 1902–1968**

Explore the life and work of John Steinbeck, the California writer who gave unforgettable voices to working people, migrants, families, and outsiders. Discover *The Grapes of Wrath*, *Of Mice and Men*, *East of Eden*, and the landscapes that shaped his fiction.

---

## 1. Who Was John Steinbeck?

John Steinbeck (1902–1968) was an American novelist, short-story writer, and journalist. Born in Salinas, California, he became one of the most important American writers of the twentieth century. His fiction often focuses on ordinary people facing poverty, injustice, loneliness, difficult choices, and the search for dignity.

Steinbeck received the **Nobel Prize in Literature in 1962**. The Nobel committee recognised his realistic and imaginative writing, sympathetic humour, and social insight.

His best-known works include *Of Mice and Men* (1937), *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939), *Cannery Row* (1945), *The Pearl* (1947), and *East of Eden* (1952).

## 2. California: The Landscape Behind the Stories

Steinbeck grew up in the Salinas Valley, an agricultural region of California. The farms, small towns, coastline, and people of this region became central to his imagination.

He worked at different jobs while studying at Stanford University, although he did not complete a degree. He later moved to New York and worked as a writer, but California remained the setting for many of his most memorable books.

His writing is closely connected with place. The landscape is not just scenery: it influences how people work, travel, survive, and relate to one another.

## 3. The Great Depression and the Dust Bowl

The Great Depression began in 1929 and brought unemployment, poverty, and economic hardship to millions of Americans. In the 1930s, severe drought and destructive dust storms also forced many farming families to leave parts of the Great Plains.

Thousands travelled west in search of work, especially to California. Migrant workers often faced low wages, insecure housing, and discrimination.

Steinbeck reported on the conditions of migrant workers in California. These experiences helped shape *The Grapes of Wrath*. The novel is fiction, but its social world is connected to real historical events.

## 4. The Grapes of Wrath

### The Joad Family's Journey

Published in 1939, *The Grapes of Wrath* follows the Joad family as they leave Oklahoma during the Dust Bowl and travel to California in search of a better life.

The journey exposes the family to uncertainty, exploitation, hunger, and loss. The novel also explores solidarity: people may become stronger when they help one another.

The book won the 1940 Pulitzer Prize for the Novel. It was adapted into a major film in 1940, directed by John Ford.

### Important Ideas

- **Displacement** — what happens when people are forced to leave their homes.
- **Dignity** — the human need to be treated with respect.
- **Solidarity** — people supporting one another in difficult conditions.
- **Injustice** — systems and actions that treat people unfairly.
- **Hope** — the belief that life can become better, even when the future is uncertain.

The novel is not simply a story about migration. It asks what a society owes to people who have lost their homes and livelihoods.

## 5. Of Mice and Men

Published in 1937, *Of Mice and Men* tells the story of George Milton and Lennie Small, two migrant ranch workers travelling through California during the Great Depression.

George is practical and protective. Lennie is physically strong but has an intellectual disability and depends on George for guidance. They share a dream of owning a small piece of land and living independently.

The novella examines friendship, loneliness, vulnerability, responsibility, and the fragile nature of dreams.

Its title comes from Robert Burns's poem *To a Mouse*. The phrase suggests that even carefully made plans can go wrong.

### George and Lennie

Their relationship is at the centre of the story. They are very different, but their companionship gives them a sense of belonging in a world where many workers live isolated lives.

When discussing the characters, avoid reducing Lennie to his strength or disability. Steinbeck presents a complicated relationship involving care, dependence, risk, and difficult moral choices.

## 6. East of Eden

Published in 1952, *East of Eden* is a family saga set largely in California's Salinas Valley. It follows two generations of the Trask family and explores rivalry, identity, guilt, choice, and the struggle between good and evil.

The novel draws on the biblical story of Cain and Abel, but Steinbeck uses the family drama to ask broader questions about human nature and personal responsibility.

One of the book's central ideas is that people are not necessarily trapped by their past. They must confront the choices available to them.

## 7. Other Important Works

- **The Red Pony (1933)** — linked stories about a boy, his family, animals, and growing up.
- **Tortilla Flat (1935)** — a humorous novel about a group of friends in Monterey, California.
- **In Dubious Battle (1936)** — a novel about labour conflict among agricultural workers.
- **Cannery Row (1945)** — a portrait of a working-class neighbourhood in Monterey, full of unusual characters and unexpected kindness.
- **The Pearl (1947)** — a short parable about a poor pearl diver whose discovery brings both hope and danger.
- **Travels with Charley (1962)** — Steinbeck's account of travelling across the United States with his dog.

## 8. Steinbeck's Writing Style

Steinbeck combined clear storytelling with social observation and emotional depth.

- **Regional detail** — places, work, weather, and local speech make the setting vivid.
- **Accessible language** — many passages use direct, concrete words.
- **Dialogue** — conversations reveal character, social position, conflict, and humour.
- **Symbolism** — objects, journeys, animals, and landscapes can carry meanings beyond the literal story.
- **Social perspective** — individual lives are often connected to wider economic and historical forces.
- **Compassion and irony** — Steinbeck could portray hardship seriously while also noticing humour and human contradiction.

His prose is readable, but the questions behind it are not simple.

## 9. Language in Context

| Word or expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| migrant worker | a person who moves to find temporary or seasonal work |
| drought | a long period with very little rain |
| dust storm | a storm in which strong winds carry large amounts of dust |
| displacement | being forced to leave one's home or usual place |
| livelihood | the way a person earns money to live |
| hardship | a difficult or painful situation |
| exploitation | unfairly using someone's work or situation for advantage |
| solidarity | support between people with a shared interest or difficulty |
| dignity | a sense of self-respect and human worth |
| to struggle | to make a difficult effort |
| to get by | to manage with the money or resources available |
| to stick together | to remain united and support one another |
| to make ends meet | to have just enough money to pay for basic needs |

**Natural American English examples:**

- A lot of families struggled to make ends meet.
- They moved west looking for work.
- The workers decided to stick together.
- It was a difficult time, but people helped each other get by.
- The story raises questions about fairness and dignity.

These are contemporary learner examples, not quotations from Steinbeck.

## 14. Your Takeaway

John Steinbeck wrote about people whose lives were shaped by work, family, place, hardship, and hope. His stories invite readers to look beyond individual events and consider the social conditions surrounding them.

As you read, notice how he describes landscapes, conversations, and small everyday details. They help reveal the larger human story.

**Read the people. Understand the place. Look beyond the surface.**
