# Walt Whitman (1819–1892)

## 1. Who Was Walt Whitman?

Walt Whitman was an American poet, essayist, and journalist whose writing helped transform modern poetry. Born on May 31, 1819, in West Hills, Long Island, he spent much of his youth in Brooklyn. Before becoming widely known as a poet, he worked as a printer, teacher, newspaper editor, and journalist.

Whitman believed that poetry could speak in a new, open voice about ordinary people, work, nature, the body, democracy, and the vast variety of American life. His language often sounds like a person thinking aloud or speaking directly to the reader.

He died in Camden, New Jersey, on March 26, 1892. The National Park Service and the Library of Congress preserve important records of his life and work.

## 2. The Book That Changed American Poetry: *Leaves of Grass*

In 1855, Whitman published the first edition of *Leaves of Grass* in Brooklyn. It was a small book containing twelve poems. The title page did not name the author; instead, the volume opened with an engraving of Whitman dressed in ordinary working clothes.

Whitman continued revising the collection throughout his life. Later editions added poems, changed titles, and rearranged the book. By the final, so-called “deathbed” edition of 1891–1892, the collection had grown to almost four hundred poems.

This continual revision is important: *Leaves of Grass* was not simply one finished book, but a lifelong poetic project.

## 3. A New Voice: Free Verse

Whitman became famous for his use of **free verse** — poetry that does not follow a regular rhyme scheme or a fixed pattern of metre. His lines can be long and expansive, often shaped by the movement of speech and thought.

Look for these features when you read him:

- **Long lines:** a sentence or thought may stretch across a whole line.
- **Lists and catalogues:** Whitman gathers people, occupations, places, and images into energetic sequences.
- **Repetition:** repeated words and sentence patterns create rhythm and emphasis.
- **Direct address:** the speaker often talks to the reader, to America, or to the natural world.
- **Democratic perspective:** workers and ordinary people receive attention alongside public figures and leaders.

Free verse does not mean that the poem has no structure. Whitman builds rhythm through repetition, parallel phrases, sound, and the movement of ideas.

## 4. Important Poems and Collections

### *Song of Myself*

One of the central works in *Leaves of Grass*. The speaker explores the self while also connecting individual experience with other people, the natural world, and the larger American community.

### “I Hear America Singing”

A short, memorable poem that presents a series of working people through the songs associated with their daily tasks. Its catalogue of occupations makes work and ordinary voices part of a shared national picture.

### “O Captain! My Captain!”

Written after the assassination of Abraham Lincoln in 1865. The poem uses the image of a ship returning safely to port while its captain has died. Its regular rhythm and rhyme make it different from much of Whitman's free verse.

### “When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom’d”

A long elegy for Lincoln that combines personal grief with images of spring, a star, and a singing bird. It is one of Whitman's major poems about death, memory, and mourning.

### *Drum-Taps*

A collection shaped by Whitman's experience of the American Civil War. During the war he visited wounded soldiers in Washington hospitals, offering practical help, writing letters, and keeping company with the men.

## 5. Whitman and the American Civil War

During the Civil War, Whitman travelled to Washington, D.C., to look for his wounded brother. He then began visiting military hospitals regularly. He brought small gifts, helped soldiers write letters, and spent time with men who were ill or dying.

His hospital experience influenced *Drum-Taps* and later poems about Lincoln. It also appears in his prose collection *Specimen Days*. Whitman's writing from this period is valuable not only as literature but also as a personal record of wartime life.

## 6. Read a Short Poem: “I Hear America Singing”

In this poem, Whitman imagines America as a chorus of individual voices. The people he names are not famous politicians or generals: they are mechanics, carpenters, masons, boatmen, shoemakers, and mothers.

As you read, notice how the repeated structure gives each person a place in the poem. Whitman does not make all the voices identical; he presents them as separate contributions to a larger sound.

**Reading task:** Find the occupations in the poem. Which verbs describe what people are doing? What picture of American life does the poem create?

The complete poem is available free through the [Poetry Foundation](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/46480/i-hear-america-singing).

## 7. English Through Whitman

Whitman's poems are useful for noticing how English creates rhythm without relying on rhyme. Pay attention to the way he joins concrete nouns, active verbs, and repeated grammatical patterns.

### Notice the language

- **I hear…** — a direct opening that places the reader inside the speaker's experience.
- **Each…** — a useful way to introduce members of a group one by one.
- **The sound of…** — a phrase for describing what can be heard or what an experience feels like.
- **As…** — often introduces a comparison or explains how something happens.

### Practice

1. In your own words, explain what free verse means.
2. Name three occupations from “I Hear America Singing”.
3. Describe one sound from your everyday environment as if it were part of a poem.
4. Write four short lines beginning with “I hear…”. Use different people, places, or sounds.

## 8. Why Read Whitman Today?

Whitman asks readers to notice the dignity of ordinary lives and the connections between individuals. His poetry celebrates variety, but it also leaves room for contradiction, loneliness, grief, and uncertainty.

For an English learner, his work offers a chance to hear a distinctive American literary voice and to study rhythm, imagery, repetition, and the language of everyday life.
