# Meet Tommy Orange: Many Voices of Contemporary Native America

**21st-Century American Writers · Tommy Orange · born 1982**

Tommy Orange is an American novelist and a citizen of the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes of Oklahoma. Born and raised in Oakland, California, he writes about Native people living in cities, family histories, identity, memory, and the ways the past continues to shape the present. His debut novel, *There There* (2018), brought a wide range of contemporary Native voices into the centre of American fiction.

---

## 1. Who Is Tommy Orange?

Tommy Orange was born in Oakland, California, in 1982. He is an enrolled member of the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes of Oklahoma. He received a bachelor's degree from Expression College in 2004 and an MFA in Creative Writing from the Institute of American Indian Arts in 2016.

Before becoming widely known as a novelist, Orange worked for several years at the Native American Health Center in Oakland. His experiences there, together with his interest in fiction and the stories of people in his community, helped shape his first novel.

His debut, *There There*, was published in 2018. It was a finalist for the 2019 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and received major literary awards, including the PEN/Hemingway Award. His second novel, *Wandering Stars*, appeared in 2024 and returns to characters and family histories connected with the first book. In 2025, Orange was named a MacArthur Fellow.

Orange's writing challenges the idea that Native American life belongs only to the past or only to rural settings. He presents Native people as contemporary individuals whose lives include cities, technology, work, art, family, tradition, and change.

## 2. There There: Twelve Lives Moving Toward One Event

*There There* is set mainly in Oakland, the city where Orange grew up. The novel follows twelve characters from Native communities. Each has a different reason for travelling to the Big Oakland Powwow, and their stories gradually intersect.

Among the characters are Jacquie Red Feather, who is trying to reconnect with her family; Dene Oxendene, who wants to honour his uncle through a documentary project; and Orvil, a teenager preparing to dance at a powwow for the first time.

The novel does not follow one hero along a single path. Instead, it moves among different lives, memories, and points of view. The reader gradually discovers how personal histories and present-day choices connect.

### What Is a Powwow?

A powwow is a gathering in which Native people come together for community, cultural celebration, dance, music, and social connection. Powwows vary across communities and nations; they are not all identical. In the novel, the Big Oakland Powwow is a central event that brings characters and storylines together.

### Why Oakland Matters

Oakland is not simply a background. Its streets, public transport, buildings, neighbourhoods, and changing urban landscape shape the characters' sense of home. The city also carries layers of Indigenous history that are often overlooked when people imagine Native life only through rural or historical images.

## 3. Many Voices: The Polyphonic Novel

*There There* is often described as a **polyphonic novel**. The term comes from music: polyphony means that several independent musical lines are heard together. In literature, a polyphonic novel gives meaningful space to multiple voices and perspectives.

This structure changes the reader's experience. A character who seems unfamiliar or difficult to understand may become clearer when another chapter reveals a different part of the story. No single narrator can explain the whole community.

**A useful reading strategy:** when the point of view changes, pause and ask:

- Whose experience are we hearing now?
- What does this character know, remember, or misunderstand?
- How does this perspective connect with an earlier chapter?
- Has the new information changed our first impression?

## 4. Themes: Identity, Memory, and Belonging

Several themes shape Orange's fiction:

- **Urban Native identity** — Native people live in cities as well as in rural communities; modern urban life is part of contemporary Native experience.
- **Memory and history** — family stories and historical events continue to influence the present.
- **Belonging** — characters ask where they feel at home and how they connect with family and community.
- **Stereotypes and representation** — the novel questions narrow images of what Native people are supposed to look like or how they are supposed to live.
- **Tradition and change** — characters negotiate inherited practices while making choices in a contemporary world.
- **Connection and survival** — individual lives are shaped by relationships, loss, resilience, and mutual support.

The novel does not offer one simple definition of Native identity. It makes room for different experiences, disagreements, generations, and ways of belonging.

## 5. Wandering Stars: A Wider Family History

Orange's second novel, *Wandering Stars* (2024), expands the history of characters and families connected to *There There*. It moves across generations and explores how historical violence, family memory, and survival can echo into the present.

The two novels can be read in conversation with each other. *There There* introduces a network of contemporary lives; *Wandering Stars* reaches further into the past and follows the consequences of history across generations.

For learners, the comparison offers a useful question: **How can a family story help us understand a larger national history?**

## 6. Language in Context

| Word or expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Native community | a community of Indigenous people |
| urban | relating to a city or town |
| powwow | a Native gathering involving community, cultural celebration, and often dance and music |
| point of view | the perspective from which a story is told |
| polyphonic | containing several distinct voices or perspectives |
| oral history | history preserved and shared through spoken accounts |
| heritage | traditions, history, and culture passed down through generations |
| identity | the qualities and experiences that shape who a person is |
| belonging | the feeling of being accepted and connected |
| to intersect | to meet or cross at a particular point |
| to challenge a stereotype | to question an oversimplified belief about a group |
| to pass something down | to give knowledge or traditions to a younger generation |
| to reconnect with | to build a relationship again |
| to carry the past forward | to continue to be influenced by earlier events |

**Natural American English examples:**

- The novel brings several different points of view together.
- Their stories intersect at a community gathering.
- She is trying to reconnect with her family.
- The book challenges stereotypes about Native people.
- Stories and traditions can be passed down through generations.
- The city is an important part of the characters' sense of belonging.

These are original learner examples, not quotations from the novels.

## 9. Put It Into Practice

### Speaking Task

Describe a place where people from different backgrounds meet. It could be a city park, a market, a library, a community centre, or a festival.

Try to use these expressions:

- a sense of belonging;
- different points of view;
- to bring people together;
- to pass traditions down.

Speak for about one minute.

### Writing Task

Write a paragraph of 100–130 words answering this question:

**How can stories help people understand a community they do not know well?**

Include one example and use at least three words from the vocabulary section.

### Discussion

A novel can show a community through many characters rather than one narrator. What might readers learn from this approach that they would miss in a story told by only one person?

## 10. Connect the Writers

Tommy Orange adds an important contemporary perspective to our American Writers collection:

- **James Fenimore Cooper** wrote about Indigenous people and frontier life in the nineteenth century. Reading him alongside Orange invites discussion about how representations of Native people have changed — and about the limits of older perspectives.
- **John Steinbeck** explored working people, economic pressure, and communities in California.
- **Percival Everett** uses satire and literary experimentation to question familiar narratives and cultural assumptions.
- **Tess Gunty** builds a multi-voiced portrait of a contemporary Midwestern community.

These writers have different backgrounds, periods, and styles. Comparing them helps learners see how American literature changes as new voices and experiences enter the conversation.

## 11. Takeaway

Tommy Orange asks readers to see Native people as part of contemporary America, not as figures frozen in the past. In *There There*, twelve lives move toward one event, but the novel's deeper subject is the complexity of identity, memory, family, and belonging. Its many voices encourage readers to listen before making assumptions.
