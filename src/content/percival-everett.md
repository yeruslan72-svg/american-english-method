# Meet Percival Everett: A New Voice in Twenty-First-Century American Literature

**21st-Century American Writers · Percival Everett · born 1956**

Discover the novelist, short-story writer, poet, painter, and professor whose work combines intellectual play, satire, humour, and sharp questions about American history. His novel *James* (2024), told from the perspective of the character Jim from Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, won the 2024 National Book Award for Fiction and the 2025 Pulitzer Prize for Fiction.

---

## 1. Who Is Percival Everett?

Percival Everett was born in 1956 at Fort Gordon, Georgia, and grew up in Columbia, South Carolina. He studied philosophy at the University of Miami and earned a master's degree in fiction from Brown University in 1982. His first novel, *Suder*, appeared in 1983.

Everett is a Distinguished Professor of English at the University of Southern California. He has written novels, short stories, poetry, and essays, and is also a painter. His fiction moves across genres: satire, mystery, western, philosophical fiction, and historical reimagining.

Everett's writing often challenges easy categories. His books can be funny and unsettling at the same time. They invite readers to question who gets to tell a story, how language shapes identity, and how American history continues to influence the present.

## 2. A Writer Who Refuses to Stay in One Genre

Everett's career began with *Suder* (1983), a comic novel about a baseball player. Since then, he has explored very different subjects and forms.

Some important works include:

- *Erasure* (2001) — a satirical novel about a Black writer, publishing expectations, and the pressure to fit narrow ideas of what a story by a Black author should be.
- *I Am Not Sidney Poitier* (2009) — a playful, philosophical novel about identity, race, and the absurdities of social labels.
- *The Trees* (2021) — a darkly comic crime novel that connects contemporary events with the history of racial violence in the American South.
- *Dr. No* (2022) — an inventive comic novel involving mathematics, wealth, and the idea of nothing.
- *James* (2024) — a reimagining of *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* from Jim's point of view.

These books show why Everett is difficult to place in a single literary category. He changes style and subject while returning to questions of power, language, identity, and freedom.

## 3. James: A Conversation with Mark Twain

Published in 2024, *James* revisits Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*. Twain's novel follows Huck, a white boy, on a journey down the Mississippi River with Jim, an enslaved man seeking freedom.

Everett changes the centre of the story. In *James*, Jim becomes the central consciousness: a father, husband, thinker, and person with a complex inner life. The novel asks readers to reconsider what the earlier story allowed Jim to say, know, and feel.

Everett has described his novel as being in conversation with Twain rather than simply correcting him. This makes *James* especially interesting beside the nineteenth-century classic: readers can compare two works, two historical moments, and two narrative perspectives.

### Why the Mississippi River Matters

The river is more than a setting. It becomes a route through danger, uncertainty, and the possibility of freedom. It also connects the novel to a major tradition in American literature: journeys that reveal the country's contradictions.

### A Note on Historical Language

The novel explores the way language can change depending on who is listening and how much danger a speaker faces. When studying this subject, distinguish between a character's strategic use of language, historical dialect, and contemporary standard English. Do not treat literary representations of enslaved people's speech as a model for imitating real people or for everyday conversation.

## 4. Erasure and the Film American Fiction

Everett's 2001 novel *Erasure* examines the publishing industry's assumptions about race and literary identity. Its protagonist, Thelonious "Monk" Ellison, is a writer who becomes frustrated when publishers appear more interested in stereotyped stories than in the work he actually wants to create.

The novel inspired the 2023 film *American Fiction*, written and directed by Cord Jefferson. The film won the Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay in 2024.

The connection between novel and film offers a useful learning opportunity: compare how a story changes when it moves from the page to the screen. Notice what can be communicated through narration in a novel and what must be shown through acting, dialogue, editing, and visual detail in a film.

## 5. The Trees: History and Satire

*The Trees* (2021) combines crime fiction, dark comedy, and historical memory. The story begins with murders in a small Mississippi town and develops into a disturbing investigation connected to the history of racial violence.

Everett uses genre conventions — detectives, clues, suspects, and investigation — while also unsettling the reader's expectations. Humour does not make the subject less serious; it can expose the absurdity and horror of what people have accepted as normal.

The novel was shortlisted for the Booker Prize in 2022.

## 6. Everett's Style

Everett's style is varied, but several features appear across his work:

- **Satire** — humour and exaggeration reveal contradictions in society.
- **Intertextuality** — a new work may speak directly or indirectly with an earlier book, myth, or cultural reference.
- **Genre play** — he borrows from crime fiction, westerns, philosophical novels, and other forms.
- **Unreliable expectations** — readers may discover that a familiar story is not as simple as it first seemed.
- **Language and power** — dialogue, naming, silence, and point of view influence who is heard and understood.
- **Intellectual humour** — jokes, wordplay, and absurd situations often carry serious ideas.

His writing can be demanding because it assumes that readers are willing to notice references, rethink assumptions, and follow changes in tone.

## 7. Language in Context

| Word or expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| point of view | the position or perspective from which a story is told |
| narrator | the voice that tells a story |
| reimagining | a new creative version of an existing story or idea |
| satire | humour or exaggeration used to criticise people or society |
| stereotype | an oversimplified idea about a group of people |
| identity | the qualities and experiences that make a person who they are |
| agency | the ability to make choices and act independently |
| assumption | something accepted as true without enough evidence |
| to challenge an idea | to question whether an idea is correct |
| to tell a story from someone's perspective | to present events as that person sees them |
| to look beneath the surface | to search for a deeper meaning |
| to turn an expectation upside down | to make something happen in an unexpected way |

**Natural American English examples:**

- The novel tells the story from a different point of view.
- The film challenges some common assumptions.
- The writer uses satire to make a serious point.
- The ending made me look beneath the surface.
- The two books describe the same world in very different ways.

These are original learner examples, not quotations from Everett.

## 9. Compare Two Literary Voices

Read Mark Twain's *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* and learn about Everett's *James*. You do not need to read both novels at once. Begin with a short, legally available excerpt and an author interview.

Think about these questions:

- Who tells the story?
- Whose thoughts and feelings are placed at the centre?
- What does the reader know about Jim in each work?
- How does the historical context affect the way each book is written?
- What changes when a familiar character becomes the narrator?

This comparison connects the nineteenth-century and twenty-first-century sections of American Writers.

## 11. Put It Into Practice

### Speaking Task

Explain the difference between a story told by an outside narrator and a story told by one of its characters. Use *James* as an example. Speak for about one minute.

### Writing Task

Write a short paragraph answering this question:

**Why does it matter who gets to tell a story?**

Try to use at least four expressions from Section 7.

### Discussion

- Can humour help people discuss difficult subjects?
- Should a modern writer be allowed to retell a classic from another character's point of view?
- What makes a literary stereotype harmful?
- How can a film adaptation change the meaning or emphasis of a novel?

## 12. Your Takeaway

Percival Everett is an important voice in twenty-first-century American literature because he questions familiar stories and makes readers examine language, identity, history, and power. *James* creates a particularly strong bridge between Mark Twain's nineteenth-century America and the questions readers continue to ask today.

**Read from another point of view. Question what seems familiar. Listen for the story behind the story.**
