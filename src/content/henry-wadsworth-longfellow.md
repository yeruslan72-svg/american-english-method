# Meet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow: The Poet Who Helped Shape American Literature

**American Writers · Henry Wadsworth Longfellow · 1807–1882**

Discover one of the most widely read American poets of the nineteenth century. His poems connect American landscapes, history, folklore, memory, and everyday people with musical rhythm and clear storytelling.

---

## 1. Who Was Henry Wadsworth Longfellow?

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow (1807–1882) was an American poet, educator, and translator. His poems became popular across the United States and were read internationally. He is remembered for *The Song of Hiawatha*, *Evangeline*, *The Courtship of Miles Standish*, “A Psalm of Life,” “The Village Blacksmith,” and “Paul Revere’s Ride.”

Longfellow brought European literary traditions into conversation with American subjects: landscapes, historical events, legends, and ordinary working people. He wrote narrative poems that tell stories, lyric poems that explore feelings and ideas, and translations that introduced English-language readers to major European literature.

## 2. From Portland to Cambridge

Longfellow was born on February 27, 1807, in Portland, Maine. His first published poem, “The Battle of Lovell’s Pond,” appeared in 1820, when he was thirteen. He entered Bowdoin College in Brunswick, Maine, and graduated in 1825.

After college, he travelled in Europe to study languages and literature. He taught at Bowdoin College from 1829 to 1835. In 1836 he moved to Cambridge, Massachusetts, to become Smith Professor of Modern Languages at Harvard. He taught French, Spanish, Italian, and German language and literature.

Longfellow lived in the historic Craigie House on Brattle Street in Cambridge. Today it is preserved as the Longfellow House–Washington’s Headquarters National Historic Site. He retired from teaching in 1854 to devote more time to writing. He died in Cambridge on March 24, 1882.

## 3. Family, Travel, and Loss

Longfellow's life included public success and personal tragedy. His first wife, Mary Storer Potter, died in 1835 while the couple were travelling in Europe. In 1843 he married Frances “Fanny” Appleton. They had six children.

In 1861, Fanny died after her dress caught fire at home. Longfellow was deeply affected by her death. His later poetry often carries a reflective awareness of memory, grief, time, and human endurance. These experiences do not explain every poem, but they help readers understand the emotional range of his work.

## 4. The Major Works

### “A Psalm of Life” (1838)

One of Longfellow's best-known early poems, “A Psalm of Life” encourages readers to live actively and meaningfully. It rejects the idea that life is merely passive waiting. Its direct message, regular rhythm, and memorable lines helped make it widely popular.

**Ideas to notice:** purpose, action, courage, time, and the influence one person can have on others.

### “The Village Blacksmith” (1840)

This poem presents a hardworking blacksmith whose strength, routine, and honesty earn the respect of his community. He is not a famous hero. He is an ordinary working person whose character is revealed through daily effort.

**Ideas to notice:** work, dignity, family, responsibility, and respect for ordinary people.

### *Evangeline: A Tale of Acadie* (1847)

This long narrative poem tells the story of Evangeline, a young Acadian woman separated from her beloved Gabriel during the forced displacement of Acadians in the eighteenth century. Her long search becomes a story about love, loss, memory, and endurance.

The poem helped bring Acadian history and landscape into popular American literature. It is a literary work, not a complete historical account of the expulsion.

**Ideas to notice:** displacement, loyalty, longing, identity, and the meaning of home.

### *The Song of Hiawatha* (1855)

This long poem draws on Indigenous North American stories and on nineteenth-century publications about Native traditions. It follows the legendary figure Hiawatha through episodes involving nature, community, love, conflict, and loss. The poem is famous for its rhythmic trochaic meter and repeated patterns.

**Read with historical awareness:** Longfellow relied heavily on published sources, especially the writings of Henry Rowe Schoolcraft. He also conflated distinct traditions and figures: Hiawatha is associated with Haudenosaunee/Iroquois history, while the figure and stories adapted in the poem draw substantially on Ojibwe and other Algonquian material. The poem is an influential nineteenth-century literary interpretation, not a reliable guide to the beliefs or traditions of any one Indigenous nation. Distinguish the poet's adaptation from Indigenous voices and scholarship.

### *The Courtship of Miles Standish* (1858)

This narrative poem is set among the early English settlers of Plymouth Colony. It presents a romantic triangle involving Miles Standish, John Alden, and Priscilla Mullins. Longfellow turned a story associated with early colonial history and family tradition into a literary narrative.

**Ideas to notice:** loyalty, love, courage, memory, and the way national stories are shaped.

### “Paul Revere’s Ride” (1860; published 1861)

This narrative poem dramatizes Paul Revere's midnight ride in April 1775, shortly before the battles of Lexington and Concord. Repetition, rhythm, sound, and suspense give the poem a strong sense of urgency.

The poem is memorable, but it should not be used as a precise historical record. Longfellow reshaped details for poetic effect and emphasized Revere's role within a larger story of resistance.

**Ideas to notice:** warning, communication, public duty, courage, and the difference between history and poetic storytelling.

### “The Wreck of the Hesperus” (1842)

A dramatic narrative poem about a ship caught in a violent winter storm. Longfellow builds tension through sound, imagery, and a steadily unfolding sequence of events.

**Ideas to notice:** danger at sea, weather, human vulnerability, and narrative suspense.

## 5. What Makes His Poetry Distinctive?

Longfellow was admired for clarity, musical rhythm, storytelling, and emotional accessibility. Many of his poems use regular meter and rhyme, making them easier to remember and read aloud.

- **Narrative movement:** a poem may have characters, a setting, a conflict, and a sequence of events.
- **Musical rhythm:** repeated beats and sound patterns help the lines stay in the reader's memory.
- **Accessible language:** many poems use clear sentences and familiar human situations, even when the subject is historical or legendary.
- **Moral reflection:** poems invite readers to think about work, courage, loyalty, compassion, or responsibility.
- **American settings:** landscapes, communities, historical events, and stories from North America appear throughout his work.

Longfellow's style belongs to the nineteenth century. Some vocabulary, grammar, and word order may sound formal or old-fashioned today. That is part of the reading experience, not a model for everyday conversation.

## 6. Language in Context

These are natural contemporary American phrases for discussing literature, not quotations from Longfellow.

### Talking About a Poem or Story

- **The poem is about…** — The poem is about courage and perseverance.
- **It tells the story of…** — It tells the story of a young woman searching for her family.
- **The poem is set in…** — The poem is set in colonial New England.
- **The speaker describes…** — The speaker describes an ordinary working day.
- **The poem explores…** — It explores loss, memory, and hope.
- **What stands out is…** — What stands out is the rhythm.
- **The imagery suggests…** — The imagery suggests danger and isolation.
- **The poem has a strong rhythm.** — The poem has a strong rhythm and regular rhyme.

### Expressing an Opinion

- I found the poem surprisingly moving.
- The language feels quite formal by today's standards.
- The story is easy to follow, but the historical context takes some explanation.
- What I liked most was the way the poem builds suspense.
- I can see why it became so popular.
- I would rather read the original than a simplified version.

### Useful Literary Vocabulary

| Word or phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| poet | a person who writes poems |
| poem | a piece of writing arranged in lines, often with rhythm or imagery |
| verse | writing arranged in metrical lines; also a line or group of lines |
| stanza | a group of lines forming a unit in a poem |
| rhyme | similarity of sounds, especially at the ends of lines |
| rhythm | the pattern of stressed and unstressed sounds |
| meter | a regular rhythmic pattern in poetry |
| imagery | descriptive language that creates pictures or sensory impressions |
| narrative poem | a poem that tells a story |
| speaker | the voice speaking in a poem |
| theme | a central idea explored in a work |
| perseverance | continued effort despite difficulty |
| to endure | to experience something difficult and continue |
| to commemorate | to honour or remember an event or person |

## 7. How to Read a Poem

You do not need to understand every old-fashioned word before you can enjoy a poem. Try this approach:

1. Read the poem once without stopping. Notice the mood and situation.
2. Read it again aloud. Listen for rhythm, repeated sounds, and pauses.
3. Identify the speaker, setting, and central event or idea.
4. Look up only the words that prevent you from understanding the meaning.
5. Notice images and comparisons. Ask what they help you imagine.
6. Summarize the poem in three or four sentences using your own English.

For a first reading, choose a short poem such as “The Village Blacksmith” or “A Psalm of Life.” Move to the longer narrative poems when you feel comfortable with the language and historical background.

## 10. Put It Into Practice

### Speaking: Introduce the Poet

Give a one-minute introduction to Henry Wadsworth Longfellow. Include where and when he lived, what he did as a teacher, two or three works he is known for, one feature of his writing, and one reason you would or would not like to read his poetry.

### Speaking: Describe a Poem

Choose a short Longfellow poem. Explain what happens or what idea it presents, who is speaking, what mood it creates, which image or line you remember, and what you think the poem means.

### Writing: A Short Response

Write 120–150 words about one of Longfellow's poems. Summarize its subject, describe its mood, and explain your reaction. You do not need to write in rhyme.

## 13. Takeaway

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow helped make poetry a central part of American cultural life. His poems connect personal experience with history, landscape, memory, and shared values. Some of his language belongs to another century, but his stories and themes still offer rich material for reading, discussion, and learning English.

Read slowly, listen to the rhythm, and use the poem as a starting point for your own thoughts in English.
