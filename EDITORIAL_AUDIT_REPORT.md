## Executive summary

## 1. Describing a Glass
**File:** `04_lessons/lesson_01_glass.md`

### Findings
- `I've got a glass...`, `It's got...`, and `if you ask me` are natural expressions that are not specifically Southern. They should not be presented as a nationwide-versus-Southern contrast.
- `mighty pretty` has a traditional Southern/regional flavour, but it should not be used to label every sentence around it as a separate Southern dialect.
- `fixin' to` is a recognisable Southern-associated conversational form; keep its regional label and do not present it as a neutral nationwide substitute for `going to`.
- `y'all` is strongly associated with the South and is also used beyond it. It addresses more than one person; avoid attaching it unnaturally to a sentence simply to make the sentence sound Southern.
- `ma'am` and `sir` are used across the United States. Their frequency and social feel vary by context and region; they are not exclusively Southern.
- Spellings such as `couldja`, `whatcha`, and `didja` are informal pronunciation respellings, not standard written forms or IPA. The lesson already warns learners about this; retain that distinction.
- Connected speech is a nationwide feature of spoken English, not a Southern-only feature.

### Changes committed
- Reframed Version B as a conversational description with a Southern touch rather than a full dialect model.
- Corrected the translation so it matches the revised English.
- Removed a misleading comparison that treated common nationwide forms as regional differences.
- Clarified that `mighty pretty` supplies the regional flavour in the sample; the rest of the wording is broadly understandable.
- Clarified the nationwide use of `ma'am` and `sir`.
- Made Southern wording optional in learner practice instead of requiring it in every attempt.

### Retain
- The visual vocabulary around facets, condensation, sunlight, and reflections.
- The progression from observation to independent description.
- The practical distinction between `smooth`, `rough`, `faceted`, and `ridged`.

## 2. At the Airport
**File:** `01_scenarios/city/airport.md`

### Findings
- The core travel language is practical and generally natural: `Can I check this bag?`, `Do you have any bags to check?`, `Where is the bag drop?`, `Could you repeat that, please?`, and `My suitcase hasn't arrived.`
- `Your bag is checked through to New York` is appropriate when the airline has tagged the bag to the final destination. The lesson already notes that this is not automatic for every connection; retain that qualification.
- TSA screening procedures vary by airport, equipment, and lane. The lesson appropriately tells learners to follow the officer's current instructions rather than memorise one universal procedure.
- The CBP section appropriately emphasises truthful, concise answers and does not promise admission based on a visa, ESTA, return ticket, or hotel booking.
- `couldja`, `whatcha`, and `didja` are pronunciation respellings of connected speech; `gonna` is a widespread informal reduction of `going to`. None is specifically Southern.
- The regional `y'all` example is placed in casual conversation with another passenger, not in formal interaction with airport staff. This is a sensible context.

### Changes committed
- Clarified the difference between informal pronunciation respellings and standard spelling.
- Clarified that these reductions are heard across the United States and are not exclusively Southern.

### Further editorial checks for a later pass
- Review the currency/customs question wording in the CBP section for precision and learner usefulness.
- Consider adding a short phrase card for `Can I check this bag?` with meaning, context, and a pronunciation note, following the language presentation standard.
- Keep the distinction between `check in`, `check a bag`, `checked bag`, and `carry-on` explicit.

## 3. At the Beach
**File:** `01_scenarios/city/beach.md`

### Findings
- The practical expressions are strong: `The water looks rough`, `Let's ask the lifeguard`, `Is it safe to swim here?`, and `I'm caught in a rip current!`
- The lesson correctly warns that beach-flag systems vary by location and that learners should read local signs and ask a lifeguard.
- In the USLA flag convention, the red-and-yellow lifeguarded swimming-area marker is one flag divided horizontally into red and yellow halves, not two separate flags stacked vertically.
- The existing rip-current guidance includes the essential message not to fight directly against the current and to seek help. A later safety review should align the full advice with current official guidance and avoid implying that swimming parallel to shore is always possible for every swimmer.
- `y'all` and `fixin' to` are appropriate examples of Southern-associated casual speech when clearly labelled as regional.
- `might as well` is a nationwide conversational expression, not a Southern-only expression; the lesson already makes that distinction.
- Connected-speech respellings should remain pronunciation aids, not standard spelling.

### Changes committed
- Corrected the red-and-yellow flag description: one horizontally divided flag, with a reminder that local systems can differ.

### Further editorial checks for a later pass
- Recheck the complete rip-current advice against current official water-safety guidance.
- Consider labelling `couldja`, `whatcha`, `didja`, and `gonna` as informal listening/pronunciation aids rather than a list of forms learners should write or imitate mechanically.

## 4. Editorial rules reinforced
1. Nationwide American English is the foundation.
2. Southern English is a regional layer, not a second mandatory version of every sentence.
3. A phrase is not Southern merely because it sounds casual.
4. Connected speech occurs across American English; respellings are aids, not standard orthography.
5. Teach active-use expressions separately from recognition-only or specialised vocabulary.
6. For travel, safety, and official procedures, distinguish natural language from claims about real-world policy.
7. Do not force regional expressions into practice tasks when the situation does not call for them.

## Second editorial pass — 13 November 2026

### Airport: currency and customs wording
- Retained the natural general question `How much money are you bringing with you?` as a possible officer question.
- Added the more precise learner-facing question: `Are you carrying more than $10,000 in currency or monetary instruments?`
- Editorial note for the site: do not imply that there is a maximum amount of money a traveler may bring. The U.S. reporting requirement concerns amounts exceeding $10,000 in currency/monetary instruments; travelers should consult current CBP instructions and applicable declaration forms. The threshold and reporting details can change, so the lesson should link to official guidance rather than present itself as legal advice.

### Beach: rip-current response
- Reworked the sequence to avoid presenting swimming parallel to shore as a universal instruction for every swimmer.
- The revised advice prioritizes staying calm, floating/treading water, not fighting the current, and signaling for help.
- Swimming parallel to shore or toward breaking waves is now conditional on the person's swimming ability and conditions.
- The advice for helping another person now explicitly says to alert a lifeguard/call 911 and throw flotation if possible; do not enter the water for an untrained rescue.

### Glass: active and recognition vocabulary
- The existing division is appropriate: broadly useful phrases such as `ice-cold`, `it's got`, `next to`, and `if you ask me` remain active-use items.
- Southern-associated or informal listening forms such as `mighty pretty`, `fixin' to`, `y'all`, and pronunciation respellings remain primarily recognition items.
- No further content change is needed in this pass. Learners may use regional forms when they understand the context, but should not be required to imitate them.

### Official reference points consulted
- NOAA / National Weather Service rip-current safety guidance.
- U.S. Customs and Border Protection guidance on currency and monetary instruments.
