# Appalachian English: Hear the Voices of the Mountains

## Learning goal

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

- understand why Appalachian English is not one uniform accent;
- recognize selected pronunciation and grammar features documented in Appalachian speech;
- understand examples such as *might could* and *a-running* without treating them as mistakes;
- listen to real speakers with attention to meaning, rhythm, and context;
- distinguish regional language features from stereotypes about Appalachian people.

**Our rule:** understand the voices; respect the speakers; keep your own English natural. You do not need to imitate an Appalachian accent.

## 1. What Do We Mean by Appalachian English?

Appalachia is a large and internally diverse region extending across parts of the eastern United States. It includes communities in states such as West Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, Virginia, North Carolina, Georgia, and Ohio. Linguists use *Appalachian English* as a broad label for related regional varieties, not for one identical way of speaking.

Geography, settlement, migration, community history, age, and social context all matter. Speech in the southern mountains of Tennessee and North Carolina will not necessarily sound the same as speech in central or northern Appalachia. Urban and rural speakers may also differ, and younger speakers do not simply reproduce the speech of older generations.

Some features associated with Appalachian English also occur elsewhere in the South or in other parts of the United States. A single pronunciation or expression cannot identify someone's home region with certainty.

## 2. Listen to Real Appalachian Voices

Start with the documentary **[Mountain Talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iHIJfbYhQFg)**, a film about language and life in the Appalachian region. It is a useful listening introduction, not a complete survey of every Appalachian community.

As you listen:

1. First, follow the meaning. Do not pause at every unfamiliar sound.
2. Notice the speaker's rhythm, stress, and intonation.
3. Replay a short passage and listen for one feature at a time.
4. Pay attention to who is speaking, where they are from, and when the recording was made.
5. Remember that a documentary edits real speech; it is not a random sample of the whole region.

For more research-based listening, explore the **[Audio-Aligned and Parsed Corpus of Appalachian English (AAPCAppE)](https://www.aapcappe.org/)**. It brings together several oral-history subcorpora, including interviews from central and eastern Kentucky, western North Carolina, Dante, Virginia, and the Great Smoky Mountains. Some collections are historical, so use the dates and speaker information as part of the listening context.

The **[Joseph Sargent Hall Collection](https://archivesofappalachia.aviaryplatform.com/collections/1560)** at East Tennessee State University's Archives of Appalachia documents language and folklore in the Great Smoky Mountains. Its catalogue describes field recordings from 1939 onward. Access to individual recordings may depend on the archive's current conditions.

## 3. Grammar You May Hear

Regional grammar is systematic. A form can differ from standardized classroom English and still be a meaningful, rule-governed part of a speaker's variety.

### Double modals: *might could*

In some Southern and Appalachian varieties, speakers use two modal verbs together:

- *We might could go tomorrow.*

In the example, *might could* expresses possibility and ability — roughly, *might be able to*. Double modals are not exclusive to Appalachia, and not every Appalachian speaker uses them.

**For active everyday English:** use *might be able to*.  
**For listening:** recognize *might could* without assuming the speaker is confused.

### A-prefixing: *a-running, a-working*

Some speakers use an *a-* prefix before certain verbs ending in *-ing*:

- *He came a-running.*
- *They were a-working all day.*

This is a documented regional grammatical feature. Its use is variable and depends on linguistic and social context; it is not added freely to every *-ing* word.

**For active everyday English:** use *running* and *working*.  
**For listening:** recognize the prefix when it appears, especially in storytelling and older recordings.

### Other patterns in historical and regional recordings

Depending on the speaker and community, you may encounter forms such as:

- *done* used to emphasize a completed action: *She's done lost it.*
- *his'n* or *your'n* as possessive forms;
- *them* used demonstratively: *them cars*;
- *was* with plural subjects in some speakers' speech: *they was there*.

These forms are not universal Appalachian features. Their frequency varies, and some are more likely in particular generations, communities, or conversational styles.

## 4. Pronunciation: Listen for Patterns, Not a Cartoon Accent

Appalachian speech has no single sound profile. Depending on location and speaker, researchers describe features that may include:

- **PRICE vowel variation:** the vowel in words such as *time* or *right* may have a different glide or a more monophthongal quality in some communities.
- **Pin–pen merger:** for some speakers, the vowels in *pin* and *pen* sound alike or very similar before nasal consonants.
- **-ing pronunciation:** the ending in *working* may be pronounced with [n] rather than [ŋ], as in many informal varieties of American English.
- **Consonant-cluster reduction:** a consonant may be less audible or absent in certain word-final clusters.

These are listening clues, not a checklist that every Appalachian speaker will match. The same feature may occur in other American regions too.

## 5. Words and Expressions: Recognition Before Imitation

Older recordings and regional glossaries preserve words that many learners will not need in everyday conversation. You may come across:

| Form | Approximate meaning | Learning priority |
|---|---|---|
| *afeared* | afraid | Recognition |
| *nary* | not any; none | Recognition |
| *liketa* | almost; nearly (in some uses) | Recognition |
| *I swan* | an expression of surprise or emphasis in some speakers' usage | Recognition |

These forms are not equally common across Appalachia, and some are strongly associated with older or particular local speech. Do not use a list of unusual words to perform a regional identity.

Expressions such as *y'all* are heard across a much wider area of the American South. They are useful to understand, but they are not uniquely Appalachian.

## 6. Mini Listening Lab

Use the documentary and archive links above.

**Task 1 — Main idea**

Listen to one short passage. Write one sentence explaining what the speaker is talking about. Focus on the message, not perfect word recognition.

**Task 2 — Sound**

Choose one word you want to replay. Is the difference mainly a vowel, a consonant, a reduced ending, or sentence rhythm?

**Task 3 — Grammar**

If you hear a form such as *might could*, *a-running*, or *done lost*, write it down. Then explain its meaning in neutral, broadly understood American English.

**Task 4 — Speaker and context**

Record the speaker's location and the date of the recording if that information is available. Is it a historical oral-history interview or a more recent recording?

**Task 5 — Avoid overgeneralizing**

Complete this sentence:

“One speaker from ______ used/heard ______. This is evidence about that speaker and context, not proof that everyone in Appalachia speaks the same way.”

## 7. Quick Check

**1. Is Appalachian English one uniform accent?**

No. It is a broad label covering varied regional and community speech patterns.

**2. Is *might could* simply bad grammar?**

No. It is a documented double-modal construction in some varieties of Southern and Appalachian English.

**3. Should learners start using *a-running* in ordinary conversation?**

Usually not. Learn to recognize it; use standard *running* in your own broadly understood everyday English.

**4. Does every Appalachian speaker use the same pronunciation features?**

No. Features vary by place, speaker, generation, and context.

**5. Does Appalachian English mean people are speaking old-fashioned or incorrect English?**

No. Regional varieties are legitimate forms of language with their own histories and patterns. They should not be used to judge a speaker's intelligence or education.

## The AEM Principle

**Hear the voices. Understand the variation. Respect the people. Speak naturally.**

Appalachian English is not a costume, a joke, or a museum piece. It is part of the living linguistic diversity of the United States.
