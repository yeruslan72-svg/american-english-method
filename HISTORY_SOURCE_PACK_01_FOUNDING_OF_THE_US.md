# AEM History Source Pack 01 — The Founding of the United States (1763–1791)

**Status:** Research pack for lesson development  
**Prepared:** 20 November 2026  
**Primary theme:** How thirteen British colonies became an independent republic, and how the founding documents shaped the United States.  
**Intended AEM use:** American History & Heritage; practical American English; reading, vocabulary, listening, discussion and cultural context.

> **Editorial rule:** Historical accuracy and permission to reproduce are separate questions. Use authoritative sources to research facts; publish only original AEM explanations and materials whose exact rights status has been checked.

## 1. Learning purpose

This package is designed to help learners:
- understand the broad sequence from colonial protest to independence and the new constitutional government;
- distinguish the Declaration of Independence, the Constitution and the Bill of Rights;
- read selected historical language without confusing eighteenth-century wording with modern everyday English;
- discuss liberty, rights, representation, government and citizenship in clear contemporary American English;
- recognize both the ideals expressed in the founding documents and the historical limits and contradictions of their application.

The lesson must not present the founding era as a simple, unanimous or universally inclusive story. The thirteen colonies differed; Loyalists and Patriots disagreed; women, enslaved people, free Black communities, Native nations and other groups experienced the period in different ways. The Declaration's language of equality coexisted with slavery and exclusions from political participation. These tensions are essential historical context, not optional footnotes.

## 2. Core chronology

| Date | Event | AEM teaching note |
|---|---|---|
| 1763 | The Seven Years' War ends; Britain seeks revenue and tighter imperial administration in North America. | Explain the postwar context; avoid suggesting one tax alone caused the Revolution. |
| 1765 | Stamp Act and colonial protests. | Introduce taxation, representation and organized protest. |
| 1770 | Boston Massacre. | Discuss contested accounts and the role of political messaging. |
| 1773 | Boston Tea Party. | Explain protest against taxation and the East India Company's tea arrangements. |
| 1774 | First Continental Congress meets. | Explain intercolonial coordination. |
| 1775 | Fighting begins at Lexington and Concord; Second Continental Congress convenes. | Distinguish the beginning of armed conflict from the later declaration of independence. |
| 4 July 1776 | Continental Congress adopts the Declaration of Independence. | Independence is formally declared; the war continues. |
| 1781 | Articles of Confederation government reaches a major milestone with ratification; British surrender at Yorktown occurs in October. | Explain the weaknesses of the first national framework without oversimplifying. |
| 1783 | Treaty of Paris formally ends the Revolutionary War. | Distinguish military victory from diplomatic recognition and later institution-building. |
| 1787 | Constitutional Convention drafts the Constitution in Philadelphia. | The Constitution is proposed as a new framework of federal government. |
| 1788 | Constitution reaches the required threshold for ratification and takes effect among ratifying states. | Explain ratification and the federal system. |
| 1789 | New federal government begins; Congress proposes amendments. | George Washington becomes the first president under the Constitution. |
| 15 December 1791 | Ten amendments are ratified as the Bill of Rights. | Explain why protections were added and what the first ten amendments cover. |

**Research check before publication:** For detailed claims, use the National Archives' milestone documents and primary-source collections. Confirm exact dates and distinguish adoption, signing, ratification, entry into force and later interpretation.

## 3. The three essential founding documents

### A. Declaration of Independence (1776)

**What it did:** Announced that the thirteen colonies regarded themselves as free and independent states and set out political reasons and principles for separation from Great Britain.

**What it did not do:** It did not create the detailed structure of the federal government and is not the Constitution. The National Archives explains that the Declaration is not legally binding in the same way as the Constitution, while remaining a powerful statement of political ideals.

**Key concepts for learners:**
- independence — freedom from another country's political control;
- unalienable rights — rights described as inherent and not legitimately removable;
- consent of the governed — the idea that government derives authority from the people;
- to dissolve political bands — to end political ties;
- self-evident — presented as obvious or requiring no proof.

**Primary sources:**
- National Archives, document overview: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration
- National Archives, transcription: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript
- National Archives, milestone document and archival citation: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/declaration-of-independence
- Library of Congress, 1911 printed edition with public-domain rights statement: https://www.loc.gov/item/11035936/

**Rights note:** National Archives high-resolution downloads of the actual Declaration parchment and the Constitution/Bill of Rights document images are expressly identified by NARA as public domain, with no permission required; NARA requests credit as the original source. This is the preferred image route for a commercial AEM lesson. Cite the exact download page and retain the credit. The National Archives' transcription can be used as a research reference; AEM should create its own explanatory text and modern-language paraphrase rather than copying a modern editorial introduction.

### B. Constitution of the United States (1787; ratified 1788)

**What it did:** Established the framework of the federal government, including its branches, powers and relationships. It replaced the Articles of Confederation as the national governing framework.

**Teaching points:**
- separation of powers: legislative, executive and judicial branches;
- checks and balances: mechanisms that limit and balance government power;
- federalism: division of authority between the national government and states;
- ratification: formal approval through the process specified for adoption.

**Primary sources:**
- National Archives, America's Founding Documents: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs
- National Archives, high-resolution founding document downloads: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/downloads

**Rights note:** NARA expressly states that its high-resolution Constitution page images are public domain and may be used without permission, with credit to the National Archives. Verify each asset on the download page and preserve its source credit.

### C. Bill of Rights (ratified 1791)

**What it did:** The first ten amendments to the Constitution set out important protections and limits on government power. The first Congress proposed twelve amendments in 1789; ten were ratified in 1791.

**Teaching points:**
- First Amendment: religion, speech, press, assembly and petition;
- Second Amendment: right to keep and bear arms (teach neutrally and distinguish constitutional text from modern legal debate);
- Fourth Amendment: protection against unreasonable searches and seizures;
- Fifth and Sixth Amendments: important protections in criminal proceedings;
- Eighth Amendment: limits on excessive bail, fines and cruel and unusual punishments;
- Tenth Amendment: powers not delegated to the United States nor prohibited to the states are reserved to the states respectively, or to the people.

**Primary sources:**
- National Archives, Bill of Rights transcription: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript
- National Archives, Bill of Rights milestone document: https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/bill-of-rights
- National Archives, high-resolution downloads: https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/downloads

**Rights note:** NARA identifies its high-resolution Bill of Rights image as public domain, with no permission required and a request for source credit. The transcription is a useful primary-text reference. Avoid treating a modern commentator's explanation as part of the original document.

## 6. AEM lesson blueprint

**Working title:** *The Birth of the United States: From Thirteen Colonies to a New Nation*

**Audience:** Adult learners at approximately A2–B2, with optional extension for advanced learners.

**Core learning sequence:**
1. **See** — view an approved image of the Declaration parchment and identify visible features: parchment, handwriting, signatures, seal (if shown).
2. **Understand** — follow a short original AEM timeline from 1763 to 1791.
3. **Describe** — use guided sentence frames to describe why the colonies sought independence and what changed after 1776.
4. **Speak** — discuss what a constitution does, why rights are written down and why historical ideals may take time to become reality.

**Suggested original AEM reading (to be written, not copied from a source):**
A clear 450–650-word overview explaining:
- why tensions grew between Britain and the colonies;
- why independence was declared in 1776;
- why winning independence did not automatically create a stable government;
- why the Constitution was drafted;
- why the Bill of Rights followed;
- who was excluded from the promises of equality and political participation at the time.

**Language focus:**
- to declare independence;
- to gain recognition;
- to reach an agreement;
- to establish a government;
- to protect individual rights;
- to share power;
- to pass / ratify an amendment;
- to have a say in government;
- to take effect.

**Speaking prompts:**
- Why did the colonies want to govern themselves?
- What is the difference between declaring independence and creating a government?
- Why might a constitution limit the power of government?
- How can a country express an ideal before that ideal is fully realized in society?

**Accuracy guardrail:** Do not teach the phrase “all men are created equal” as proof that equality existed in 1776. Explain it as a founding ideal whose application was sharply limited and contested. Include slavery, the status of women, Native nations and political exclusions in historically grounded language appropriate to the learner's level.
